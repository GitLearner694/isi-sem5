0. Notation and standing conventions

Data are pairs \((X_1,Y_1),\dots,(X_n,Y_n)\). Throughout, the predictors \(X_i\) are treated as fixed (non-random) constants; all randomness is in the errors. Write

$$\bar X=\tfrac1n\sum_i X_i,\quad \bar Y=\tfrac1n\sum_i Y_i,$$ $$S_{xx}=\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2,\quad S_{xy}=\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y),\quad S_{yy}=\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i-\bar Y)^2 .$$

All sums run \(i=1,\dots,n\) unless said otherwise. Two identities used constantly (both from \(\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)=0\)):

$$S_{xx}=\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)X_i=\sum_i X_i^2-n\bar X^2,\qquad S_{xy}=\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)Y_i=\sum_i X_iY_i-n\bar X\bar Y. \tag{0.1}$$
Why (0.1) holds \(\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)\bar X=\bar X\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)=0\), so \(\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)(X_i-\bar X)=\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)X_i\); expanding the right side gives \(\sum X_i^2-\bar X\sum X_i=\sum X_i^2-n\bar X^2\). Identically, \(\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)\bar Y=0\) turns \(S_{xy}\) into \(\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)Y_i=\sum X_iY_i-\bar X\sum Y_i=\sum X_iY_i-n\bar X\bar Y\).

1.1 Model, least squares and the normal equations

Model Simple linear regression $$Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1 X_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad i=1,\dots,n, \tag{1}$$ where \(\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_n\) are uncorrelated with \(E(\varepsilon_i)=0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i)=\sigma^2\) (constant; the homoscedastic case). Equivalently, \(E(Y\mid X=X_i)=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=X_i)=\sigma^2\). More generally one allows \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i)=\sigma_i^2\) depending on \(X_i\) (heteroscedastic; taken up in §4).

Interpretation (scatter-plot reading). In narrow vertical strips around each \(X\)-value, the centres of the \(Y\)-points fall (about) on a line of slope \(\beta_1\), intercept \(\beta_0\); and the spreads in the strips are (about) equal — this is exactly homoscedasticity.

Least squares and the normal equations

Least squares chooses \((b_0,b_1)\) to minimise the sum of squared vertical distances

$$Q(b_0,b_1)=\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i-b_0-b_1X_i)^2 .$$
Result 1 Normal equations Any minimiser \((b_0,b_1)\) of \(Q\) satisfies $$n\,b_0+b_1\sum_i X_i=\sum_i Y_i, \tag{2}$$ $$b_0\sum_i X_i+b_1\sum_i X_i^2=\sum_i X_iY_i. \tag{3}$$
Proof \(Q\) is a quadratic in \((b_0,b_1)\). Differentiate: $$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial b_0}=-2\sum_i (Y_i-b_0-b_1X_i),\qquad \frac{\partial Q}{\partial b_1}=-2\sum_i X_i(Y_i-b_0-b_1X_i).$$ Setting both to zero and rearranging gives (2) and (3). These are genuine minimisers, not saddle points: the Hessian is $$\nabla^2 Q=2\begin{pmatrix}n & \sum X_i\\ \sum X_i & \sum X_i^2\end{pmatrix},$$ whose determinant is \(2^2\big(n\sum X_i^2-(\sum X_i)^2\big)=4n\,S_{xx}>0\) (by (0.1)), with positive top-left entry, so the Hessian is positive definite whenever \(S_{xx}>0\), i.e. whenever the \(X_i\) are not all equal. A convex quadratic with positive-definite Hessian has a unique global minimiser.
Result 2 Least-squares estimates Provided \(S_{xx}>0\), the unique solution of (2)–(3) is $$\boxed{\,b_1=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}=\frac{\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)}{\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)^2} =\frac{\sum_i X_iY_i-n\bar X\bar Y}{\sum_i X_i^2-n\bar X^2},\qquad b_0=\bar Y-b_1\bar X.\,} \tag{4}$$
Proof Divide (2) by \(n\): \(b_0+b_1\bar X=\bar Y\), i.e. \(b_0=\bar Y-b_1\bar X\). Substitute into (3): $$(\bar Y-b_1\bar X)\sum X_i+b_1\sum X_i^2=\sum X_iY_i \;\Longrightarrow\; b_1\Big(\sum X_i^2-\bar X\sum X_i\Big)=\sum X_iY_i-\bar Y\sum X_i.$$ The left bracket is \(\sum X_i^2-n\bar X^2=S_{xx}\); the right side is \(\sum X_iY_i-n\bar X\bar Y=S_{xy}\) (both by (0.1)). Hence \(b_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}\). The two displayed forms of \(b_1\) coincide by (0.1). \(b_0\) then follows.

The fitted line is \(\widehat Y=b_0+b_1X\); the fitted values are \(\widehat Y_i=b_0+b_1X_i\), used to predict \(Y\) at a given \(X\). The estimates are also written \(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1\).

The linear-representation lemma (the engine)

Almost every moment computation below is a one-line consequence of the following.

Lemma A \(b_1\) is a fixed linear combination of the \(Y_i\) With weights \(c_i:=\dfrac{X_i-\bar X}{S_{xx}}\), $$b_1=\sum_i c_i Y_i,\qquad \text{and}\qquad \sum_i c_i=0,\quad \sum_i c_iX_i=1,\quad \sum_i c_i^2=\frac{1}{S_{xx}} . \tag{A}$$
Proof By (0.1), \(S_{xy}=\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)Y_i\), so \(b_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}=\sum_i c_iY_i\). Then \(\sum_i c_i=\frac1{S_{xx}}\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)=0\); \(\sum_i c_iX_i=\frac1{S_{xx}}\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)X_i=\frac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx}}=1\) (using (0.1)); and \(\sum_i c_i^2=\frac1{S_{xx}^2}\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)^2=\frac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx}^2}=\frac1{S_{xx}}\).

Because the \(X_i\) are fixed, the \(c_i\) are constants; \(b_1\) is “linear in \(Y\).” The intercept \(b_0=\bar Y-b_1\bar X=\sum_i\big(\tfrac1n-\bar X c_i\big)Y_i\) is linear in \(Y\) too. From (A) we read off, with no distributional assumption beyond (1):

Corollary A1 First two moments of \(b_1\) \(E(b_1)=\beta_1\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(b_1)=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\).
Proof \(E(b_1)=\sum_i c_iE(Y_i)=\sum_i c_i(\beta_0+\beta_1X_i)=\beta_0\underbrace{\textstyle\sum c_i}_{0} +\beta_1\underbrace{\textstyle\sum c_iX_i}_{1}=\beta_1\). Since the \(Y_i\) are uncorrelated with common variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\operatorname{Var}(b_1)=\sum_i c_i^2\operatorname{Var}(Y_i)=\sigma^2\sum_i c_i^2 =\sigma^2/S_{xx}\).

The seven residual identities

Residuals are \(e_i=Y_i-\widehat Y_i=Y_i-(b_0+b_1X_i)\). Since \(\varepsilon_i=Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\), the \(e_i\) estimate the \(\varepsilon_i\). The handout lists seven properties; each is proved below.

Result 3 Properties of the fitted line and residuals
  1. \(\sum_i e_i=0\).
  2. \(\sum_i e_i^2\le \sum_i (Y_i-u_0-u_1X_i)^2\) for all \((u_0,u_1)\), equality iff \((u_0,u_1)=(b_0,b_1)\).
  3. \(\sum_i \widehat Y_i=\sum_i Y_i\).
  4. \(\sum_i X_ie_i=0\).
  5. \(\sum_i \widehat Y_i e_i=0\).
  6. The line passes through \((\bar X,\bar Y)\).
  7. \(b_1=r_{XY}\,s_Y/s_X\), where \(r_{XY}\) is the sample correlation and \(s_X,s_Y\) the sample SDs.
Proof

(1) The first normal equation (2) says \(\sum_i(Y_i-b_0-b_1X_i)=0\), i.e. \(\sum_i e_i=0\).

(4) The second normal equation (3) says \(\sum_i X_i(Y_i-b_0-b_1X_i)=0\), i.e. \(\sum_i X_ie_i=0\).

(2) This is the defining property of least squares: \((b_0,b_1)\) minimises \(Q(u_0,u_1)=\sum_i (Y_i-u_0-u_1X_i)^2\), and by Result 1 the minimiser is unique, so the inequality is strict off \((b_0,b_1)\). (\(\sum_i e_i^2=Q(b_0,b_1)\).)

(3) \(\sum_i \widehat Y_i=\sum_i(Y_i-e_i)=\sum_i Y_i-\sum_i e_i=\sum_i Y_i\) by (1).

(5) \(\sum_i \widehat Y_ie_i=\sum_i(b_0+b_1X_i)e_i=b_0\sum_i e_i+b_1\sum_i X_ie_i=0\) by (1) and (4).

(6) \(b_0=\bar Y-b_1\bar X\) rearranges to \(\bar Y=b_0+b_1\bar X\): the point \((\bar X,\bar Y)\) satisfies the line equation.

(7) With \(s_X^2=\tfrac{1}{n-1}S_{xx}\), \(s_Y^2=\tfrac1{n-1}S_{yy}\), \(r_{XY}=\dfrac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}\sqrt{S_{yy}}}\), $$r_{XY}\frac{s_Y}{s_X}=\frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}\sqrt{S_{yy}}}\cdot \frac{\sqrt{S_{yy}}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}=b_1.$$

Read (1) and (6) together. Property (1), \(\sum e_i=0\), holds because the model has an intercept — column \(\mathbf 1\) is in the design. Drop the intercept and \(\sum e_i\ne0\) in general. Property (6) is the same fact geometrically.

SSE and the identity \(E(\mathrm{SSE})=(n-2)\sigma^2\)

Result 4 Computing form of SSE $$\mathrm{SSE}:=\sum_i e_i^2=\sum_i (Y_i-\bar Y)^2-b_1^2\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)^2=S_{yy}-b_1^2S_{xx}. \tag{4a}$$
Proof By (6), \(\widehat Y_i-\bar Y=b_1(X_i-\bar X)\), so \(e_i=Y_i-\widehat Y_i=(Y_i-\bar Y)-b_1(X_i-\bar X)\). Squaring and summing, $$\mathrm{SSE}=\sum_i (Y_i-\bar Y)^2-2b_1\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)+b_1^2\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)^2 =S_{yy}-2b_1S_{xy}+b_1^2S_{xx}.$$ Since \(S_{xy}=b_1S_{xx}\), the middle term is \(2b_1^2S_{xx}\), giving \(S_{yy}-b_1^2S_{xx}\).
Result 5 Unbiased estimation of \(\sigma^2\) \(E(\mathrm{SSE})=(n-2)\sigma^2\). Hence \(\displaystyle \widehat\sigma^2=\mathrm{MSE}:=\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n-2}\) is unbiased for \(\sigma^2\). The divisor \(n-2\) is the error degrees of freedom: sample size \(n\) minus the number of estimated regression coefficients (\(\beta_0,\beta_1\)).
Proof By (4a), \(\mathrm{SSE}=S_{yy}-b_1^2S_{xx}\), and \(S_{xx}\) is a constant, so \(E(\mathrm{SSE})=E(S_{yy})-S_{xx}E(b_1^2)\).

Term 1. Write \(Y_i-\bar Y=\beta_1(X_i-\bar X)+(\varepsilon_i-\bar\varepsilon)\) (the \(\beta_0\)'s cancel). Then $$S_{yy}=\sum_i(Y_i-\bar Y)^2=\beta_1^2S_{xx}+2\beta_1\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)(\varepsilon_i-\bar\varepsilon) +\sum_i(\varepsilon_i-\bar\varepsilon)^2 .$$ Taking expectations: the cross term has \(E=0\) (each \(E\varepsilon_i=0\)); and \(E\sum_i(\varepsilon_i-\bar\varepsilon)^2=(n-1)\sigma^2\) — the standard fact that the sum of squared deviations of \(n\) uncorrelated, equal-variance, mean-zero variables has expectation \((n-1)\sigma^2\) [proof: \(E\sum\varepsilon_i^2=n\sigma^2\), \(E(n\bar\varepsilon^2)=n\cdot\sigma^2/n=\sigma^2\), and \(\sum(\varepsilon_i-\bar\varepsilon)^2=\sum\varepsilon_i^2-n\bar\varepsilon^2\)]. So \(E(S_{yy})=\beta_1^2S_{xx}+(n-1)\sigma^2\).

Term 2. By Corollary A1, \(E(b_1^2)=\operatorname{Var}(b_1)+(Eb_1)^2=\sigma^2/S_{xx}+\beta_1^2\), so \(S_{xx}E(b_1^2)=\sigma^2+\beta_1^2S_{xx}\).

Subtracting: \(E(\mathrm{SSE})=\beta_1^2S_{xx}+(n-1)\sigma^2-\sigma^2-\beta_1^2S_{xx}=(n-2)\sigma^2.\)

1.2 Moments and variances of the estimators

Result 6 Unbiasedness and variances Under (1), with \(X_i\) fixed, $$E(b_0)=\beta_0,\quad E(b_1)=\beta_1,\quad E(\widehat\sigma^2)=\sigma^2,$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(b_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(b_0)=\sigma^2\!\left(\frac1n+\frac{\bar X^2}{S_{xx}}\right),\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(b_0,b_1)=-\frac{\sigma^2\bar X}{S_{xx}} . \tag{7}$$ For the mean response \(\widehat Y_h=b_0+b_1X_h\) at a value \(X_h\), $$E(\widehat Y_h)=\beta_0+\beta_1X_h,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\widehat Y_h)=\sigma^2\!\left(\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{S_{xx}}\right). \tag{9a}$$ Replacing \(\sigma^2\) by \(\mathrm{MSE}\) gives the estimated variances \(s^2(b_1),s^2(b_0),s^2(\widehat Y_h)\); their square roots are the estimated standard errors.
Proof \(E(b_1)=\beta_1\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(b_1)=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\) are Corollary A1; \(E(\widehat\sigma^2)=\sigma^2\) is Result 5.

A useful covariance. \(\operatorname{Cov}(\bar Y,b_1)=\operatorname{Cov}\big(\tfrac1n\sum_j Y_j,\sum_i c_iY_i\big) =\tfrac1n\sum_i c_i\operatorname{Var}(Y_i)=\tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\sum_i c_i=0\) (uncorrelated \(Y\)'s; \(\sum c_i=0\)).

\(b_0\). \(b_0=\bar Y-b_1\bar X\), so \(E(b_0)=E(\bar Y)-\bar X E(b_1) =(\beta_0+\beta_1\bar X)-\bar X\beta_1=\beta_0\). Its variance: $$\operatorname{Var}(b_0)=\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y)+\bar X^2\operatorname{Var}(b_1)-2\bar X\operatorname{Cov}(\bar Y,b_1) =\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\bar X^2\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}-0 =\sigma^2\Big(\frac1n+\frac{\bar X^2}{S_{xx}}\Big).$$

\(\operatorname{Cov}(b_0,b_1)\). \(\operatorname{Cov}(b_0,b_1)=\operatorname{Cov}(\bar Y-b_1\bar X,\,b_1) =\operatorname{Cov}(\bar Y,b_1)-\bar X\operatorname{Var}(b_1)=0-\bar X\sigma^2/S_{xx}.\)

\(\widehat Y_h\). Write \(\widehat Y_h=b_0+b_1X_h=\bar Y+b_1(X_h-\bar X)\) (using \(b_0=\bar Y-b_1\bar X\)). Then \(E(\widehat Y_h)=(\beta_0+\beta_1\bar X)+\beta_1(X_h-\bar X)=\beta_0+\beta_1X_h\), and since \(\operatorname{Cov}(\bar Y,b_1)=0\), $$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat Y_h)=\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y)+(X_h-\bar X)^2\operatorname{Var}(b_1) =\sigma^2\Big(\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{S_{xx}}\Big).$$

Geometry of the two variance formulas. \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat Y_h)\) is smallest at \(X_h=\bar X\) (where it equals \(\sigma^2/n\)) and grows quadratically as \(X_h\) moves away — the fitted line is “pinned” most tightly at the centre of the data. And \(\operatorname{Var}(b_1)=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\) shrinks as the \(X_i\) spread out: more spread in the predictor buys a better-determined slope.

1.3 The normal model: MLE and exact distributions

Add to (1) the assumption that \(\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_n\) are i.i.d. \(N(0,\sigma^2)\). Then \(Y_i\mid X_i\sim N(\beta_0+\beta_1X_i,\ \sigma^2)\) independently — the normal linear regression model.

Maximum likelihood

Result 7 MLE = least squares The maximum-likelihood estimators of \(\beta_0,\beta_1\) equal the least-squares estimators \(b_0,b_1\), while \(\displaystyle \widehat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\frac1n\sum_i (Y_i-b_0-b_1X_i)^2=\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n} =\frac{n-2}{n}\,\mathrm{MSE}.\)
Proof The likelihood is $$L(\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2)=\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i)^2\Big) =(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}Q(\beta_0,\beta_1)\Big),$$ so \(\log L=-\tfrac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}Q(\beta_0,\beta_1)\). For any fixed \(\sigma^2>0\), \(\log L\) is maximised over \((\beta_0,\beta_1)\) by minimising \(Q\) — that is precisely least squares, giving \(b_0,b_1\). Substituting \(Q(b_0,b_1)=\mathrm{SSE}\) and setting \(\partial\log L/\partial\sigma^2=-\tfrac{n}{2\sigma^2}+\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^4}\mathrm{SSE}=0\) yields \(\widehat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\mathrm{SSE}/n\). (Note the MLE of \(\sigma^2\) is biased low by the factor \((n-2)/n\); \(\mathrm{MSE}\) corrects it.)

Exact sampling distributions

These are the results that make testing possible. The clean route is the matrix picture. Collect \(Y=(Y_1,\dots,Y_n)^\top\), the \(n\times2\) design \(\mathbf X=[\mathbf 1\ \ X]\) (columns \(\mathbf 1\) and \((X_1,\dots,X_n)^\top\)), \(\beta=(\beta_0,\beta_1)^\top\). Model (1) is \(Y=\mathbf X\beta+\varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2 I_n)\). The LS estimator is \(b=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top Y\), the hat matrix is \(H=\mathbf X(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\), fitted \(\widehat Y=HY\), residuals \(e=(I-H)Y\).

Result 8 The four distributional facts Under the normal model,
  1. \(b=(b_0,b_1)^\top\sim N_2\big(\beta,\ \sigma^2(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\big)\); in scalar form the covariance matrix has entries exactly (7).
  2. \(\mathrm{SSE}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-2}\).
  3. \(b\) and \(\mathrm{SSE}\) are independent.
  4. \(\dfrac{b_i-\beta_i}{s(b_i)}\sim t_{\,n-2}\) for \(i=0,1\).
Proof

(1) \(b\) is a fixed linear map of the Gaussian vector \(Y\), hence Gaussian, with \(E(b)=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top \mathbf X\beta=\beta\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(b)=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top(\sigma^2I)\mathbf X(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1} =\sigma^2(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\). For SLR, $$\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X=\begin{pmatrix}n&\sum X_i\\ \sum X_i&\sum X_i^2\end{pmatrix},\quad (\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}=\frac{1}{nS_{xx}}\begin{pmatrix}\sum X_i^2&-\sum X_i\\ -\sum X_i&n\end{pmatrix},$$ since \(\det(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)=n\sum X_i^2-(\sum X_i)^2=nS_{xx}\). The \((2,2)\) entry is \(n/(nS_{xx})=1/S_{xx}\); the \((1,1)\) entry is \(\sum X_i^2/(nS_{xx})=(S_{xx}+n\bar X^2)/(nS_{xx}) =\tfrac1n+\tfrac{\bar X^2}{S_{xx}}\); the off-diagonal is \(-\sum X_i/(nS_{xx})=-\bar X/S_{xx}\). Multiplying by \(\sigma^2\) reproduces (7).

(2) \(H\) is symmetric idempotent (\(H^2=H\)) with \(\operatorname{rank}H=\operatorname{tr}H=\operatorname{tr}\big((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\big) =\operatorname{tr}(I_2)=2\); hence \(I-H\) is symmetric idempotent of rank \(n-2\). Also \((I-H)\mathbf X=0\), so \((I-H)Y=(I-H)(\mathbf X\beta+\varepsilon)=(I-H)\varepsilon\), giving \(\mathrm{SSE}=e^\top e=\varepsilon^\top(I-H)\varepsilon\). Diagonalise \(I-H=P\Lambda P^\top\) with \(P\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(1,\dots,1,0,0)\) (\(n-2\) ones). Put \(Z=P^\top\varepsilon/\sigma\sim N(0,I_n)\). Then \(\mathrm{SSE}/\sigma^2=Z^\top\Lambda Z=\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}Z_j^2\sim\chi^2_{n-2}\), a sum of \(n-2\) independent squared standard normals. (This is the Fisher–Cochran mechanism in miniature.)

(3) \(b-\beta=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\varepsilon\) and \(e=(I-H)\varepsilon\) are jointly Gaussian, and $$\operatorname{Cov}(b,e)=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top(\sigma^2I)(I-H) =\sigma^2(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\big(\mathbf X^\top-\mathbf X^\top H\big)=0,$$ because \(\mathbf X^\top H=\mathbf X^\top\). Uncorrelated + jointly Gaussian \(\Rightarrow\) independent; and \(\mathrm{SSE}=e^\top e\) is a function of \(e\), so \(b\perp\mathrm{SSE}\).

(4) By (1), \(\dfrac{b_i-\beta_i}{\sigma\sqrt{[(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}]_{ii}}}\sim N(0,1)\); call it \(U\). By (2), \(V:=\mathrm{SSE}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-2}\), and by (3) \(U\perp V\). Hence, by the definition of Student's \(t\), $$\frac{U}{\sqrt{V/(n-2)}} =\frac{(b_i-\beta_i)/\big(\sigma\sqrt{[(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}]_{ii}}\big)} {\sqrt{\mathrm{SSE}/\big(\sigma^2(n-2)\big)}} =\frac{b_i-\beta_i}{\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}\,[(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}]_{ii}}} =\frac{b_i-\beta_i}{s(b_i)}\sim t_{\,n-2}.$$ The \(\sigma\)'s cancel, leaving the observable standard error \(s(b_i)\).

This is the whole toolbox. Every confidence interval and \(t\)-test in §2 is the pivot \((b_i-\beta_i)/s(b_i)\sim t_{n-2}\); every \(F\)-statistic in §1.4 and §2.1 is a ratio of the independent \(\chi^2\)'s from Result 8(2) and its ANOVA companion below.

1.4 The ANOVA approach

Split the total variability of \(Y\) into a part explained by the predictor and a part left in the errors.

Result 9 ANOVA decomposition $$\underbrace{\sum_i (Y_i-\bar Y)^2}_{\mathrm{SSTO}} =\underbrace{\sum_i (\widehat Y_i-\bar Y)^2}_{\mathrm{SSR}} +\underbrace{\sum_i (Y_i-\widehat Y_i)^2}_{\mathrm{SSE}}, \qquad \mathrm{SSR}=b_1^2S_{xx}. \tag{13}$$
Proof Write \(Y_i-\bar Y=(\widehat Y_i-\bar Y)+(Y_i-\widehat Y_i)=(\widehat Y_i-\bar Y)+e_i\). Square and sum: $$\mathrm{SSTO}=\sum_i(\widehat Y_i-\bar Y)^2+\sum_i e_i^2+2\sum_i(\widehat Y_i-\bar Y)e_i.$$ The cross term vanishes: \(\sum_i(\widehat Y_i-\bar Y)e_i=\sum_i\widehat Y_ie_i-\bar Y\sum_i e_i=0-0=0\) by residual properties (5) and (1). This gives \(\mathrm{SSTO}=\mathrm{SSR}+\mathrm{SSE}\). Finally, by property (6) \(\widehat Y_i-\bar Y=b_1(X_i-\bar X)\), so \(\mathrm{SSR}=\sum_i b_1^2(X_i-\bar X)^2=b_1^2S_{xx}\).
Result 10 Degrees of freedom, expected mean squares, distributions $$\mathrm{d.f.}(\mathrm{SSTO})=n-1,\quad \mathrm{d.f.}(\mathrm{SSR})=1,\quad \mathrm{d.f.}(\mathrm{SSE})=n-2,$$ and these add: \((n-1)=1+(n-2)\). With \(\mathrm{MSR}=\mathrm{SSR}/1\), \(\mathrm{MSE}=\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)\), $$E(\mathrm{SSE})=(n-2)\sigma^2,\qquad E(\mathrm{SSR})=\sigma^2+\beta_1^2S_{xx},$$ $$E(\mathrm{MSE})=\sigma^2,\qquad E(\mathrm{MSR})=\sigma^2+\beta_1^2S_{xx}. \tag{10a}$$ Under the normal model, and under \(H_0:\beta_1=0\), $$\frac{\mathrm{SSR}}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{1},\qquad \frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-2}, \qquad\text{independently.}$$
Proof

Expected mean squares. \(E(\mathrm{SSE})=(n-2)\sigma^2\) is Result 5. For \(\mathrm{SSR}=b_1^2S_{xx}\), using \(E(b_1^2)=\sigma^2/S_{xx}+\beta_1^2\), \(E(\mathrm{SSR})=S_{xx}E(b_1^2)=\sigma^2+\beta_1^2S_{xx}\); divide by the respective d.f. for the mean squares.

Degrees of freedom. \(\mathrm{SSTO}=\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2=Y^\top(I-\tfrac1n J)Y\) with \(I-\tfrac1n J\) idempotent of rank \(n-1\) (\(J=\mathbf 1\mathbf1^\top\)); \(\mathrm{SSE}=Y^\top(I-H)Y\), rank \(n-2\); and \(\mathrm{SSR}=Y^\top(H-\tfrac1n J)Y\). Since \(\mathbf 1\) lies in the column space of \(\mathbf X\), \(H\tfrac1nJ=\tfrac1nJ\) and \(H-\tfrac1nJ\) is idempotent of rank \(2-1=1\). The ranks add just as the matrices do: \((I-\tfrac1nJ)=(H-\tfrac1nJ)+(I-H)\).

Distributions under \(H_0\). When \(\beta_1=0\), \(b_1\sim N(0,\sigma^2/S_{xx})\), so \(\dfrac{\mathrm{SSR}}{\sigma^2}=\dfrac{b_1^2S_{xx}}{\sigma^2}=\Big(\dfrac{b_1}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/S_{xx}}}\Big)^2 =\big(N(0,1)\big)^2\sim\chi^2_1\). And \(\mathrm{SSE}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-2}\) by Result 8(2). They are independent because \(\mathrm{SSR}\) is a function of \(b\) (namely \(b_1^2S_{xx}\)) and \(b\perp\mathrm{SSE}\) by Result 8(3).

1.5 \(R^2\) and association

Definition Coefficient of determination $$R^2=\frac{\mathrm{SSR}}{\mathrm{SSTO}}=1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SSTO}},\qquad R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=1-\frac{\mathrm{MSE}}{\mathrm{MSTO}},\ \ \mathrm{MSTO}=\frac{\mathrm{SSTO}}{n-1}.$$
Result 11 Properties of \(R^2\) \(0\le R^2\le 1\); \(R^2=\big(\operatorname{Corr}(X,Y)\big)^2\), with \(\operatorname{Corr}(X,Y)\) the signed square root (sign of \(b_1\)); and \(R^2\) is the proportion of \(Y\)-variability explained by the regression, unit-free.
Proof The two equalities in the definition are the ANOVA identity (13) divided by \(\mathrm{SSTO}\). Since \(\mathrm{SSR}\ge0\) and \(\mathrm{SSE}\ge0\) and they sum to \(\mathrm{SSTO}\), each ratio is in \([0,1]\), so \(0\le R^2\le1\). For the correlation identity, \(\mathrm{SSTO}=S_{yy}\) and \(\mathrm{SSR}=b_1^2S_{xx}=\big(S_{xy}/S_{xx}\big)^2S_{xx}=S_{xy}^2/S_{xx}\), hence $$R^2=\frac{\mathrm{SSR}}{\mathrm{SSTO}}=\frac{S_{xy}^2/S_{xx}}{S_{yy}}=\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}} =\big(r_{XY}\big)^2 .$$ The sign of \(r_{XY}\) is the sign of \(S_{xy}\), which is the sign of \(b_1\). “Unit-free” because \(r_{XY}\) is scale-invariant. \(R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}\) replaces the sums of squares by their mean squares, penalising for the \(1\) d.f. spent on the slope; it can decrease when a useless predictor is added.

2. Inference: intervals, tests, bands

All of this section rests on Result 8: \((b_i-\beta_i)/s(b_i)\sim t_{n-2}\), and its analogues for \(\widehat Y_h\) and predictions.

Confidence intervals for \(\beta_0,\beta_1\)

Result 12 A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) two-sided CI for \(\beta_i\) (\(i=0,1\)) is $$\big(b_i-t(1-\tfrac\alpha2;n-2)\,s(b_i),\ \ b_i+t(1-\tfrac\alpha2;n-2)\,s(b_i)\big),$$ where \(t(1-\tfrac\alpha2;n-2)\) is the \((1-\tfrac\alpha2)\)-quantile of \(t_{n-2}\).
Proof Let \(t^\ast=t(1-\tfrac\alpha2;n-2)\). By symmetry of \(t_{n-2}\) and Result 8(4), \(P\!\big(-t^\ast\le \tfrac{b_i-\beta_i}{s(b_i)}\le t^\ast\big)=1-\alpha\). The event inside is \(\{\,b_i-t^\ast s(b_i)\le \beta_i\le b_i+t^\ast s(b_i)\,\}\); this is the pivot method. Because \(s(b_i)\) is observable, the endpoints are computable.

Tests for \(\beta_0,\beta_1\)

Result 13 \(t\)-tests of \(H_0:\beta_i=\beta_{i0}\) With test statistic \(T_i=\dfrac{b_i-\beta_{i0}}{s(b_i)}\), which is \(t_{n-2}\) under \(H_0\):
AlternativeReject \(H_0\) at level \(\alpha\) when\(p\)-value
\(\beta_i\gt\beta_{i0}\)\(T_i\gt t(1-\alpha;n-2)\)\(P(t_{n-2}\gt T_i^{\text{obs}})\)
\(\beta_i\lt\beta_{i0}\)\(T_i\lt t(\alpha;n-2)\)\(P(t_{n-2}\lt T_i^{\text{obs}})\)
\(\beta_i\ne\beta_{i0}\)\(|T_i|\gt t(1-\tfrac\alpha2;n-2)\)\(P(|t_{n-2}|\gt|T_i^{\text{obs}}|)\)
Proof Under \(H_0:\beta_i=\beta_{i0}\), \(T_i=(b_i-\beta_{i0})/s(b_i)\sim t_{n-2}\) by Result 8(4). Each rejection region is chosen so that, under \(H_0\), its probability is exactly \(\alpha\): e.g. for the upper-tailed case \(P(t_{n-2}>t(1-\alpha;n-2))=\alpha\). The \(p\)-value is by definition the null probability of a statistic at least as extreme (in the alternative's direction) as the observed \(T_i^{\text{obs}}\), and one rejects iff \(p<\alpha\) — equivalent to the critical-value rule.

Inference for the mean response \(E(Y_h)\) at \(X=X_h\)

Result 14 \(\dfrac{\widehat Y_h-E(Y_h)}{s(\widehat Y_h)}\sim t_{n-2}\), where \(s^2(\widehat Y_h)=\mathrm{MSE}\big(\tfrac1n+\tfrac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{S_{xx}}\big)\). Hence a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI for \(E(Y_h)=\beta_0+\beta_1X_h\) is \(\widehat Y_h\pm t(1-\tfrac\alpha2;n-2)\,s(\widehat Y_h)\).
Proof \(\widehat Y_h=(1,X_h)\,b\) is linear in the Gaussian \(b\), hence normal, with mean \(\beta_0+\beta_1X_h\) and variance (9a). So \(\big(\widehat Y_h-E(Y_h)\big)/\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat Y_h)}\sim N(0,1)\). This is a function of \(b\), independent of \(\mathrm{SSE}\) (Result 8(3)); dividing by \(\sqrt{\mathrm{SSE}/(\sigma^2(n-2))}\) turns \(\sigma^2\) into \(\mathrm{MSE}\) and produces \(t_{n-2}\) exactly as in Result 8(4). The interval is the pivot.

Prediction of a new observation \(Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}\)

Result 15 Prediction interval For a new draw \(Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}=\beta_0+\beta_1X_h+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{new}}\) at \(X_h\), independent of the fitting data, the prediction error \(Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}-\widehat Y_h\) has $$\operatorname{Var}\big(Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}-\widehat Y_h\big) =\sigma^2\Big(1+\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{S_{xx}}\Big),$$ and \(\dfrac{Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}-\widehat Y_h}{s(Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}-\widehat Y_h)}\sim t_{n-2}\), giving the \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) prediction interval \(\widehat Y_h\pm t(1-\tfrac\alpha2;n-2)\,s(Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}-\widehat Y_h)\).
Proof \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{new}}\) is independent of \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\), hence of \(\widehat Y_h\). Therefore $$\operatorname{Var}(Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}-\widehat Y_h)=\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{new}}) +\operatorname{Var}(\widehat Y_h)=\sigma^2+\sigma^2\Big(\tfrac1n+\tfrac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{S_{xx}}\Big) =\sigma^2\Big(1+\tfrac1n+\tfrac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{S_{xx}}\Big).$$ The difference is normal (both terms Gaussian), mean \(0\); estimating \(\sigma^2\) by \(\mathrm{MSE}\) (independent of the numerator, being a function of the fitting residuals) yields \(t_{n-2}\). The extra “\(1+\)” over Result 14 is the irreducible variance of a single future observation — prediction intervals are always wider than confidence intervals for the mean.

Working–Hotelling confidence band for the whole line

Result 16 The band \(\ \widehat Y_h\pm w\,s(\widehat Y_h)\ \) with \(\ w^2=2\,F(1-\alpha;2,n-2)\ \) contains the true line \(E(Y_h)=\beta_0+\beta_1X_h\) simultaneously for all \(X_h\), with probability \(1-\alpha\).
Proof Let \(a=(1,X_h)^\top\), so \(\widehat Y_h-E(Y_h)=a^\top(b-\beta)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat Y_h)=\sigma^2 a^\top(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}a\). By Cauchy–Schwarz in the inner product \(\langle u,v\rangle=u^\top(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}v\)'s dual, $$\sup_{a\ne0}\frac{\big(a^\top(b-\beta)\big)^2}{a^\top(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}a} =(b-\beta)^\top(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)(b-\beta).$$ Now \((b-\beta)\sim N_2(0,\sigma^2(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1})\), so \((b-\beta)^\top(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)(b-\beta)/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_2\); it is independent of \(\mathrm{SSE}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-2}\) (Result 8(3)). Therefore $$\frac{\sup_{X_h}\big(\widehat Y_h-E(Y_h)\big)^2/s^2(\widehat Y_h)}{2} =\frac{(b-\beta)^\top(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)(b-\beta)/(2\sigma^2)}{\mathrm{SSE}/(\sigma^2(n-2))} \sim F_{2,n-2}.$$ Requiring this \(\le F(1-\alpha;2,n-2)\) — an event of probability \(1-\alpha\) — is exactly \(\big(\widehat Y_h-E(Y_h)\big)^2\le 2F(1-\alpha;2,n-2)\,s^2(\widehat Y_h)\) for all \(X_h\), i.e. \(|\widehat Y_h-E(Y_h)|\le w\,s(\widehat Y_h)\) with \(w^2=2F(1-\alpha;2,n-2)\).

2.1 Testing a linear hypothesis (the \(F\)-test)

View \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) as a comparison of nested models. The full model is \(Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\varepsilon_i\); under \(H_0\) the reduced model is \(Y_i=\beta_0+\varepsilon_i\), whose least-squares fit is \(\widehat Y_i=\bar Y\).

Result 17 The general \(F\)-statistic and \(F=T^2\) With \(\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{full}}=\mathrm{SSE}\) (d.f. \(n-2\)) and \(\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{red}}=\mathrm{SSTO}\) (d.f. \(n-1\)), and \(\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{red}}-\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{full}}=\mathrm{SSR}\), $$F=\frac{(\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{red}}-\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{full}})/(\,\mathrm{df}_{\text{red}}-\mathrm{df}_{\text{full}}\,)} {\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{full}}/\mathrm{df}_{\text{full}}} =\frac{\mathrm{SSR}/1}{\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)}=\frac{\mathrm{MSR}}{\mathrm{MSE}}\ \overset{H_0}{\sim}\ F_{1,n-2},$$ and this \(F\) equals the square of the two-sided \(t\)-statistic \(T=b_1/s(b_1)\) for \(H_0:\beta_1=0\).
Proof Under the reduced model the fitted value is \(\bar Y\), so \(\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{red}}=\sum_i(Y_i-\bar Y)^2=\mathrm{SSTO}\); under the full model \(\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{full}}=\sum_i(Y_i-\widehat Y_i)^2=\mathrm{SSE}\). Their difference is \(\mathrm{SSTO}-\mathrm{SSE}=\mathrm{SSR}\) by (13), on \((n-1)-(n-2)=1\) d.f. Under \(H_0\) and normality, \(\mathrm{SSR}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_1\) independent of \(\mathrm{SSE}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-2}\) (Result 10), so the ratio of mean squares is \(F_{1,n-2}\) by definition of the \(F\)-law. Finally, $$T^2=\frac{b_1^2}{s^2(b_1)}=\frac{b_1^2}{\mathrm{MSE}/S_{xx}}=\frac{b_1^2S_{xx}}{\mathrm{MSE}} =\frac{\mathrm{SSR}}{\mathrm{MSE}}=\frac{\mathrm{MSR}}{\mathrm{MSE}}=F,$$ using \(s^2(b_1)=\mathrm{MSE}/S_{xx}\) and \(\mathrm{SSR}=b_1^2S_{xx}\), \(\mathrm{MSR}=\mathrm{SSR}/1\). So the \(F\)-test of \(\beta_1=0\) and the two-sided \(t\)-test are identical (consistent with \(t_{n-2}^2=F_{1,n-2}\)).

\(Y=\) selling price (\(\$1000\)), \(X=\) size (\(100\) sq ft). Summary statistics:

$$n=19,\quad \bar X=15.719,\quad \bar Y=75.211,\quad S_{xx}=40.805,\quad S_{yy}=556.078,\quad S_{xy}=120.001.$$
Example Every number worked and checked

Slope, intercept. \(b_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}=120.001/40.805=2.941\); \(b_0=\bar Y-b_1\bar X=75.211-2.941(15.719)=28.98\). Fitted line \(\widehat Y=28.98+2.941X\).

Prediction. At \(X_h=18.5\) (a 1850 sq ft house), \(\widehat Y_h=28.98+2.941(18.5)=83.39\) (\(\$83{,}390\)).

ANOVA / \(\sigma^2\). \(\mathrm{SSR}=b_1^2S_{xx}=352.91\), \(\mathrm{SSE}=S_{yy}-\mathrm{SSR}=556.08-352.91=203.17\), d.f. \(=n-2=17\), \(\mathrm{MSE}=203.17/17=11.95\).

Standard errors. \(s^2(b_1)=\mathrm{MSE}/S_{xx}=11.95/40.805=0.2929\), \(s(b_1)=0.5412\); \(s^2(b_0)=\mathrm{MSE}(\tfrac1{19}+\tfrac{15.719^2}{40.805})=73.00\), \(s(b_0)=8.544\).

4. Regression diagnostics

Fitting a line does not certify that a line is appropriate. Assumptions can fail, and a few extreme points can dominate the fit. Diagnostics are mostly graphical, but two exact algebraic facts — the influence-update for the slope, and the residual identities — deserve proof.

4.1 Diagnostics for the predictor; influence of an extreme \(X\)

Stem-and-leaf, dot, box, and (for time-ordered data) sequence plots reveal the range, concentration, and outliers of \(X\). The reason \(X\)-outliers matter is quantified by a leave-one-out identity.

Result 18 How one added point moves the slope Let \(b_1^{(n-1)}\) be the LS slope from the first \(n-1\) points and \(b_0^{(n-1)}\) the corresponding intercept. Let \(e_n^{(n-1)}=Y_n-\big(b_0^{(n-1)}+b_1^{(n-1)}X_n\big)\) be the residual of the \(n\)-th point from that fit. Then the full-data slope satisfies $$b_1=b_1^{(n-1)}+\frac{X_n-\bar X}{S_{xx}}\,e_n^{(n-1)} =b_1^{(n-1)}+\Big(1-\tfrac1n\Big)\frac{(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})}{S_{xx}}\,e_n^{(n-1)}, \tag{18a}$$ where \(\bar X,S_{xx}\) are computed from all \(n\) points and \(\bar X_{n-1}\) from the first \(n-1\).
Proof Use the update rules for the corrected sums when the point \((X_n,Y_n)\) is appended (each a one-line consequence of the definitions): $$S_{xx}=S_{xx}^{(n-1)}+\tfrac{n-1}{n}(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})^2,\qquad S_{xy}=S_{xy}^{(n-1)}+\tfrac{n-1}{n}(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})(Y_n-\bar Y_{n-1}). \tag{$\dagger$}$$ Also \(\bar X-\bar X_{n-1}=\tfrac1n(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})\), so \(X_n-\bar X=(1-\tfrac1n)(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})\). Now $$b_1=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}=\frac{S_{xy}^{(n-1)}+\tfrac{n-1}{n}(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})(Y_n-\bar Y_{n-1})}{S_{xx}}.$$ Add and subtract \(b_1^{(n-1)}S_{xx}=b_1^{(n-1)}\big(S_{xx}^{(n-1)}+\tfrac{n-1}{n}(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})^2\big) =S_{xy}^{(n-1)}+b_1^{(n-1)}\tfrac{n-1}{n}(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})^2\) in the numerator (using \(S_{xy}^{(n-1)}=b_1^{(n-1)}S_{xx}^{(n-1)}\)): $$b_1-b_1^{(n-1)}=\frac{\tfrac{n-1}{n}(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})\big[(Y_n-\bar Y_{n-1})-b_1^{(n-1)}(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})\big]}{S_{xx}}.$$ The bracket is \((Y_n-\bar Y_{n-1})-b_1^{(n-1)}(X_n-\bar X_{n-1}) =Y_n-\big(\bar Y_{n-1}-b_1^{(n-1)}\bar X_{n-1}\big)-b_1^{(n-1)}X_n =Y_n-b_0^{(n-1)}-b_1^{(n-1)}X_n=e_n^{(n-1)}\). Substituting and using \(\tfrac{n-1}{n}(X_n-\bar X_{n-1})=X_n-\bar X\) gives (18a).
Numbers An outlying \(X\) drags the slope

First \(n-1=15\) points: \(\sum X_i=90,\ \sum Y_i=330,\ \sum X_i^2=1000,\ \sum X_iY_i=2400\), so \(\bar X_{15}=6,\ \bar Y_{15}=22\), \(S_{xy}^{(15)}=2400-15(6)(22)=420,\ S_{xx}^{(15)}=1000-15(6^2)=460\), giving \(b_1^{(15)}=420/460=0.913\) and \(b_0^{(15)}=22-0.913(6)=16.52\).

Append \((X_n,Y_n)=(22,22)\), a point far out in \(X\). Then \(\bar X=112/16=7\), \(\bar Y=352/16=22\), \(S_{xy}=2400+22\cdot22-16(7)(22)=420\), and \(S_{xx}=1000+22^2-16(7^2)=1484-784=700\). The full slope is $$b_1=\frac{420}{700}=0.600 .$$

Check via the update (18a). \(e_n^{(15)}=22-(16.52+0.913\cdot22)=-14.61\), and $$b_1^{(15)}+\frac{X_n-\bar X}{S_{xx}}e_n^{(15)}=0.913+\frac{22-7}{700}(-14.61)=0.913-0.313=0.600\ \checkmark$$ One high-leverage point pulled the slope from \(0.913\) down to \(0.600\).

Correction to the handout's arithmetic here. The handout prints \(S_{xx}=616\) and hence \(b_1=420/616=0.682\). That value of \(S_{xx}\) corresponds to \(X_n^2=400\) (i.e. \(X_n=20\)), which contradicts \(X_n=22\) used everywhere else in the same example (and needed for \(\bar X=7\)). With the consistent \(X_n=22\) one gets \(S_{xx}=1484-784=700\) and \(b_1=0.600\), which the influence formula (18a) reproduces exactly. The qualitative point — a lone extreme \(X\) sharply changes the slope — is unaffected. The leverage flag \(X_n>\bar X_{n-1}+2\,s_{X(n-1)}\) still holds: \(s_{X(15)}=\sqrt{460/14}=5.73\) and \(22>6+2(5.73)=17.46\).

4.2 Diagnostics for residuals

Result 19 Sample behaviour of residuals
  1. Mean: \(\bar e=\tfrac1n\sum_i e_i=0\).
  2. Sample variance: \(s^2:=\tfrac{1}{n-2}\sum_i (e_i-\bar e)^2=\tfrac{1}{n-2}\sum_i e_i^2 =\mathrm{MSE}\).
  3. Zero sample correlations: \(\operatorname{Corr}(X,e)=0\) and \(\operatorname{Corr}(\widehat Y,e)=0\).
  4. Non-independence: the \(e_i\) are dependent even when the \(\varepsilon_i\) are independent, because they satisfy the two linear constraints \(\sum_i e_i=0\) and \(\sum_i X_ie_i=0\).
Proof

(1) is residual property (1). (2): since \(\bar e=0\), \(\sum_i(e_i-\bar e)^2=\sum_i e_i^2=\mathrm{SSE}\), so \(s^2=\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)=\mathrm{MSE}\). (3): sample covariance of \(X\) and \(e\) is \(\propto\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)(e_i-\bar e)=\sum_i X_ie_i-\bar X\sum_i e_i=0\) by properties (4),(1); likewise \(\sum_i(\widehat Y_i-\overline{\widehat Y})(e_i-\bar e) =\sum_i\widehat Y_ie_i-\overline{\widehat Y}\sum_ie_i=0\) by (5),(1). (4): the residual vector lives in the \((n-2)\)-dimensional space orthogonal to the two columns of \(\mathbf X\) — the identities \(\sum e_i=0,\ \sum X_ie_i=0\) are those two orthogonality constraints, so the \(e_i\) cannot be freely independent. In matrix form \(\operatorname{Cov}(e)=\sigma^2(I-H)\), which is non-diagonal; the \(e_i\) are correlated. For large \(n\) the off-diagonal entries \(-\sigma^2h_{ij}\) are small and the residuals behave almost independently.

Two different “variances,” don't conflate them. Result 19(2) is the sample variance of the residual numbers, and it estimates \(\sigma^2\). The true variance of an individual residual is \(\operatorname{Var}(e_i)=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii})\) with \(h_{ii}=\tfrac1n+\tfrac{(X_i-\bar X)^2}{S_{xx}}\) (the \(i\)-th diagonal of \(H\)) — not constant across \(i\). High-leverage points (large \(h_{ii}\)) have smaller-variance residuals: the line is pulled toward them, hiding their influence. This is exactly why studentised residuals divide by \(\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}(1-h_{ii})}\).

Definition Semi-studentised residual \(\displaystyle e_i^\ast=\frac{e_i-\bar e}{\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}}=\frac{e_i}{\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}}\) (since \(\bar e=0\)). It rescales residuals to be roughly unit-variance. (The fully studentised residual \(e_i/\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}(1-h_{ii})}\) accounts for the leverage correction above and is preferable for outlier flagging.)

4.3 Diagnostic plots — what each departure looks like

Residual plots (residuals vs predictor or vs fitted values) and the normal Q–Q plot expose the ways the model can fail. The six departures the handout lists:

  1. the regression function is not linear;
  2. the error variance is not constant;
  3. the errors are not independent;
  4. the model fits all but a few outliers;
  5. the errors are not normal;
  6. a needed predictor has been omitted.
Reading the plots

Nonlinearity. If residuals-vs-\(X\) show a systematic curved pattern, a straight line is inadequate. (Handout's simulation: true \(Y=5-X+0.1X^2+\varepsilon\); the fitted line has huge \(R^2=0.989\), yet the residuals-vs-\(X\) plot is a clear U-shape — a reminder that high \(R^2\) does not validate linearity.)

Outliers. Flag observation \(i\) if its (semi-)studentised residual is large, rule of thumb \(|e_i^\ast|>3\).

Non-normality. The normal probability (Q–Q) plot orders the residuals and plots the \(k\)-th smallest \(e_{(k)}\) against its expected position under normality, \(\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}\;z\!\big(\tfrac{k-0.375}{n+0.25}\big)\), where \(z(q)\) is the \(q\)-quantile of \(N(0,1)\). If the errors are normal the points lie near the diagonal; systematic curvature signals skewness, and an S-shape signals heavy tails. (Handout's four simulations, \(Y=2+3X+\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)\), \(t_5\), left-skew, right-skew, produce respectively a straight Q–Q line, a heavy-tailed S, and the two skew signatures.)

Heteroscedasticity. If the residual spread widens or narrows along \(X\), the equal-variance assumption fails — common in financial data, or when observations come from strata of differing precision. (Handout's simulation makes \(\log\sigma^2(X)=1+0.1X\), and the residuals-vs-\(X\) plot fans out to the right.)

The plotting-position constant. Using \(\tfrac{k-0.375}{n+0.25}\) rather than the naive \(\tfrac{k}{n}\) is a continuity/bias correction (Blom's positions): it keeps the extreme order statistics off the boundaries \(0,1\), where the normal quantile \(z(\cdot)\) blows up, and makes the expected plotted points nearly unbiased for the true normal quantiles.

Everything here recurs in matrix generality: the normal equations become \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\,b=\mathbf X^\top Y\); Lemma A and the variance formulas become \(\operatorname{Cov}(b)=\sigma^2(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\); Result 8 is the Fisher–Cochran theory of quadratic forms; the ANOVA \(F\) and the linear-hypothesis \(F\) become the general \(F\)-test for \(H_0:\mathbf C\beta=0\). The projection lemmas doing the work in §1.3 are proved in the companion note “OLS Revision & the Gauss–Markov Framework.”

Prepared for the Semester 5 dashboard, following Debashis Paul's handout “Overview of Simple Linear Regression.” One arithmetic slip in the handout's §4.1 example is flagged and corrected above; all other results are reproduced and proved as stated. Corrections welcome.
