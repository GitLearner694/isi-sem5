1. Setup, notation and the two model regimes

Throughout, \(Y\) is an \(n\times 1\) random vector of observations, \(X\) is a known \(n\times p\) design or model matrix, \(\beta\) is a \(p\times 1\) vector of unknown parameters, and \(e\) is an \(n\times 1\) vector of unobservable errors. The linear model is

$$Y = X\beta + e, \qquad \mathrm{E}(e) = 0.$$

Write \(r \equiv r(X)\) for the rank of \(X\). Two regimes matter, and conflating them is the single most common source of confusion in this course:

RegimeConditionConsequence
Full rank\(r(X) = p\) (columns linearly independent) \(X'X\) is invertible; \(\hat\beta\) is unique; every \(\lambda'\beta\) is estimable
Less than full rank\(r(X) < p\) \(X'X\) singular; \(\hat\beta\) not unique; only some \(\lambda'\beta\) are estimable

Regression models are usually full rank. ANOVA models written in the natural “overparametrised” way — \(y_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_i + e_{ij}\) — are usually not, and that is exactly why the course invests so much in generalized inverses and estimability. Christensen's strategy, which the notes below follow, is to develop everything in terms of the column space \(C(X)\) rather than in terms of \(\beta\), because \(C(X)\) is well defined in both regimes while \(\beta\) is not.

Notation \(C(A)\) denotes the column space of \(A\) (the span of its columns), \(r(A)\) its rank, \(A'\) its transpose, \(\mathrm{tr}(A)\) its trace. \(C(A)^\perp\) is the orthogonal complement of \(C(A)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). \(I\) is the identity, \(J = \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}'\) the matrix of ones, and \(\mathbf{1}\) the vector of ones. For a possibly singular square \(A\), \(A^-\) denotes any generalized inverse (§2.2).

2. Linear algebra toolkit

Everything in Weeks 1–2 rests on three facts: what a column space is, what a generalized inverse does, and what an orthogonal projection is. This section is the revision that the rest of the course quietly assumes.

2.1 Column spaces and rank

\(C(X) = \{X b : b \in \mathbb{R}^p\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\). The model says \(\mathrm{E}(Y) = X\beta \in C(X)\): the mean vector is somewhere in the column space, and estimation is the business of deciding where.

Two rank facts used repeatedly:

  • \(C(X'X) = C(X')\) and hence \(r(X'X) = r(X)\).
  • \(\dim C(X) = r\), so \(\dim C(X)^\perp = n - r\). This is where the residual degrees of freedom come from.
Why \(C(X'X)=C(X')\) \(C(X'X) \subseteq C(X')\) is immediate. For the reverse, it is enough to show the null spaces agree. If \(X'Xb = 0\) then \(b'X'Xb = \|Xb\|^2 = 0\), so \(Xb = 0\), so \(X'Xb=0 \Rightarrow X b = 0\). Hence \(N(X'X) = N(X)\), and by rank–nullity \(r(X'X) = r(X)\). Combined with the inclusion, the spaces coincide.

2.2 Generalized inverses

Definition Generalized inverse \(A^-\) is a generalized inverse of \(A\) if \(A A^- A = A\). Such a matrix always exists, and is not unique unless \(A\) is invertible (in which case \(A^- = A^{-1}\)).

The only facts needed now:

  • If \(A\) is symmetric and \(A^-\) is a generalized inverse, so is \((A^-)'\).
  • Linear system \(Ab = c\) is consistent iff \(c \in C(A)\), and then \(b = A^-c\) is a solution.

The point of generalized inverses in this course is narrow: they let us write down a solution of the normal equations when \(X'X\) is singular. Every quantity we actually care about will turn out not to depend on which generalized inverse was used — that is the content of Theorem 2.2 below.

2.3 Perpendicular projection operators

Definition Perpendicular projection operator \(M\) is the perpendicular projection operator (ppo) onto \(C(X)\) if
  1. \(Mv = v\) for every \(v \in C(X)\), and
  2. \(Mw = 0\) for every \(w \in C(X)^\perp\).
Theorem 2.1 Existence, uniqueness, characterisation The ppo onto \(C(X)\) exists and is unique. A matrix \(M\) is that ppo if and only if \(M\) is symmetric, \(M^2 = M\), and \(C(M) = C(X)\).
Proof

Uniqueness. Every \(Y \in \mathbb{R}^n\) decomposes uniquely as \(Y = v + w\) with \(v\in C(X)\), \(w \in C(X)^\perp\). Conditions (1) and (2) force \(MY = v\), which pins \(M\) down completely. So at most one such \(M\) exists.

Existence. Put \(M = X(X'X)^-X'\) and let \(G = (X'X)^-\). We first show the key identity

$$X G X' X = X. \tag{$\ast$}$$

Let \(A = XGX'X - X\). Since \(X'X\) is symmetric, both \(G\) and \(G'\) are generalized inverses of \(X'X\), so \(X'XGX'X = X'X\) and \(X'XG'X'X = X'X\). Expanding,

$$A'A = X'XG'X'XGX'X - X'XG'X'X - X'XGX'X + X'X = X'X - X'X - X'X + X'X = 0 .$$

A real matrix with \(A'A=0\) is zero, giving \((\ast)\), i.e. \(MX = X\).

\(M\) is symmetric. Transposing \((\ast)\) gives \(X'XG'X' = X'\); applying \((\ast)\) with the generalized inverse \(G'\) in place of \(G\) and transposing gives \(X'XGX' = X'\). Hence

$$M'M = XG'X'XGX' = XG'(X'XGX') = XG'X' = M' .$$

But \(M'M\) is symmetric for any \(M\), so \(M'\) is symmetric, i.e. \(M' = M\).

\(M\) is idempotent. \(M^2 = (MX)GX' = XGX' = M\), using \(MX=X\).

\(C(M)=C(X)\). \(M = XGX'\) gives \(C(M)\subseteq C(X)\); \(MX = X\) gives \(C(X) \subseteq C(M)\).

Finally, such an \(M\) satisfies the definition: if \(v = Xb \in C(X)\) then \(Mv = MXb = Xb = v\); if \(w \perp C(X)\) then \(X'w = 0\) so \(Mw = XGX'w = 0\).

Theorem 2.2 Invariance \(M = X(X'X)^-X'\) is the same matrix for every choice of generalized inverse \((X'X)^-\).
Proof Each such matrix satisfies the definition of the ppo onto \(C(X)\) by Theorem 2.1, and the ppo is unique.

This is the pivotal structural fact of the whole subject: the fitted values do not depend on any arbitrary choice. \(\hat\beta\) may be non-unique, but \(M\), and therefore \(\hat Y = MY\), is completely determined by the model.

Corollary 2.3 \(I - M\) is the ppo onto \(C(X)^\perp\). Consequently \(M(I-M) = 0\), \(X'(I-M) = 0\), and \(r(I-M) = n-r\).
Proof \(I-M\) is symmetric and \((I-M)^2 = I - 2M + M^2 = I-M\). If \(w\perp C(X)\), \((I-M)w = w\); if \(v \in C(X)\), \((I-M)v = 0\). So \(I-M\) is the ppo onto \(C(X)^\perp\). Then \(M(I-M) = M - M^2 = 0\), and \(X'(I-M) = X' - X'M = X' - (MX)' = X'-X' = 0\). Since \(I-M\) is idempotent, \(r(I-M) = \mathrm{tr}(I-M) = n - \mathrm{tr}(M) = n-r\).
Fact worth memorising. For an idempotent matrix, rank equals trace. Applied to \(M\): \(\mathrm{tr}(M) = r(M) = r(X) = r\). Almost every degrees-of-freedom count in the course is this identity in disguise.

3. Random vectors, covariance matrices and quadratic forms

For a random vector \(Y\) with \(\mathrm{E}(Y) = \mu\), the covariance matrix is \(\mathrm{Cov}(Y) = \mathrm{E}[(Y-\mu)(Y-\mu)']\). The two rules used constantly:

$$\mathrm{E}(AY + b) = A\mu + b, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(AY+b) = A\,\mathrm{Cov}(Y)\,A' .$$

The following lemma is the workhorse behind every unbiasedness calculation for variance estimators, and behind the ANOVA table later in the course.

Lemma 3.1 Expectation of a quadratic form If \(\mathrm{E}(Y) = \mu\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(Y) = V\), then for any conformable \(A\), $$\mathrm{E}(Y'AY) = \mathrm{tr}(AV) + \mu'A\mu .$$ Note this requires no distributional assumption — only the first two moments.
Proof \(Y'AY\) is a scalar, so \(Y'AY = \mathrm{tr}(Y'AY) = \mathrm{tr}(AYY')\). Taking expectations and using linearity of trace, $$\mathrm{E}(Y'AY) = \mathrm{tr}\big(A\,\mathrm{E}(YY')\big) = \mathrm{tr}\big(A(V + \mu\mu')\big) = \mathrm{tr}(AV) + \mathrm{tr}(A\mu\mu') = \mathrm{tr}(AV) + \mu'A\mu ,$$ the last step because \(\mathrm{tr}(A\mu\mu') = \mathrm{tr}(\mu'A\mu) = \mu'A\mu\) for the scalar \(\mu'A\mu\).

4. Least squares: the revision core

4.1 The problem and its geometry

Least squares chooses \(\beta\) to minimise the squared distance between \(Y\) and \(X\beta\):

$$Q(\beta) = \|Y - X\beta\|^2 = (Y-X\beta)'(Y-X\beta).$$

Note this is a purely geometric criterion — no probability has entered yet. That is worth holding onto: least squares is a projection; the Gauss–Markov theorem (§7) is the separate statistical claim that this projection is optimal.

Theorem 4.1 Least squares is projection \(Q(\beta)\) is minimised at \(\hat\beta\) if and only if \(X\hat\beta = MY\). The minimised value is \(\|(I-M)Y\|^2\).
Proof Decompose, inserting \(MY\): $$Y - X\beta = (Y - MY) + (MY - X\beta) = (I-M)Y + (MY - X\beta).$$ The second term lies in \(C(X)\) (both \(MY\) and \(X\beta\) do), while the first lies in \(C(X)^\perp\). Their inner product therefore vanishes, and $$\|Y-X\beta\|^2 = \|(I-M)Y\|^2 + \|MY - X\beta\|^2 .$$ The first term does not involve \(\beta\); the second is \(\ge 0\) and equals \(0\) exactly when \(X\beta = MY\). Since \(MY \in C(X)\), such a \(\beta\) exists.

So the least squares fitted vector is \(\hat Y = MY\), the point of \(C(X)\) closest to \(Y\), and the residual vector is

$$\hat e = Y - \hat Y = (I-M)Y .$$

4.2 The normal equations

Differentiating \(Q(\beta) = Y'Y - 2\beta'X'Y + \beta'X'X\beta\) and setting the gradient to zero:

$$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial \beta} = -2X'Y + 2X'X\beta = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \boxed{\,X'X\hat\beta = X'Y\,}$$
Theorem 4.2 The normal equations are always consistent, and \(\hat\beta\) solves them if and only if \(X\hat\beta = MY\). One solution is \(\hat\beta = (X'X)^-X'Y\); it is unique iff \(r(X)=p\), in which case \(\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'Y\).
Proof Consistency. \(X'Y \in C(X') = C(X'X)\), so a solution exists. Equivalence. If \(X\hat\beta = MY\) then \(X'X\hat\beta = X'MY = (MX)'Y = X'Y\). Conversely if \(X'X\hat\beta = X'Y\) then \(X'(Y - X\hat\beta) = 0\), so \(Y - X\hat\beta \in C(X)^\perp\); writing \(Y = X\hat\beta + (Y-X\hat\beta)\) as a sum of an element of \(C(X)\) and an element of \(C(X)^\perp\) and applying \(M\) gives \(MY = X\hat\beta\). Uniqueness. Solutions are unique iff \(X'X\) is nonsingular iff \(r(X'X) = r(X) = p\).
The conceptual split to carry into Week 2. \(\hat Y = MY\) is always unique; \(\hat\beta\) need not be. So statements about the model should be phrased in terms of \(\hat Y\), or in terms of estimable functions \(\lambda'\beta\) (§6), never in terms of \(\hat\beta\) as such.

4.3 Algebraic properties of residuals

These are all consequences of Corollary 2.3 and require no assumptions beyond the definition of least squares. This is exactly the “algebraic properties of residuals” item on the course syllabus.

Proposition 4.3 With \(\hat e = (I-M)Y\) and \(\hat Y = MY\):
  1. \(X'\hat e = 0\) — residuals are orthogonal to every column of \(X\).
  2. If \(\mathbf{1} \in C(X)\) (the model has an intercept), \(\sum_i \hat e_i = 0\).
  3. \(\hat Y' \hat e = 0\) — fitted values are uncorrelated with residuals in the sample.
  4. \(\|Y\|^2 = \|\hat Y\|^2 + \|\hat e\|^2\) (Pythagoras).
Proof (1) \(X'\hat e = X'(I-M)Y = 0\) by Corollary 2.3. (2) If \(\mathbf 1 \in C(X)\) it is a linear combination of columns of \(X\), so \(\mathbf 1' \hat e = 0\), which is \(\sum_i \hat e_i = 0\). (3) \(\hat Y'\hat e = Y'M(I-M)Y = 0\). (4) \(Y = MY + (I-M)Y\) with orthogonal summands.
Read (2) carefully. Residuals sum to zero because there is an intercept, not as a general law. Fit a no-intercept model and \(\sum \hat e_i \ne 0\) in general — a standard exam trap.

4.4 Example: simple linear regression

Example 4.4 Deriving the familiar formulas

Take \(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + e_i\), so \(X = [\mathbf 1 \;\; x]\) and \(\beta=(\beta_0,\beta_1)'\). Then

$$X'X = \begin{pmatrix} n & \sum x_i \\ \sum x_i & \sum x_i^2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad X'Y = \begin{pmatrix} \sum y_i \\ \sum x_i y_i \end{pmatrix}.$$

The normal equations are

$$n\hat\beta_0 + \Big(\textstyle\sum x_i\Big)\hat\beta_1 = \textstyle\sum y_i, \qquad \Big(\textstyle\sum x_i\Big)\hat\beta_0 + \Big(\textstyle\sum x_i^2\Big)\hat\beta_1 = \textstyle\sum x_i y_i .$$

The first gives \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x\) — which is just property 4.3(2) rewritten. Substituting into the second and writing \(S_{xx} = \sum (x_i-\bar x)^2\), \(S_{xy} = \sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)\),

$$\hat\beta_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \qquad \hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x .$$

Here \(r(X) = 2 = p\) provided the \(x_i\) are not all equal, so the solution is unique. If all \(x_i\) are equal, \(r(X)=1\): the columns \(\mathbf 1\) and \(x\) are proportional, \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) are individually meaningless, and only \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\) is estimable — a miniature preview of §6.

5. The Gauss–Markov framework

Week 2 begins here. So far only geometry has been used. We now impose second-moment assumptions — and nothing more.

Definition Gauss–Markov assumptions The model \(Y = X\beta + e\) satisfies the Gauss–Markov (GM) assumptions if $$\mathrm{E}(e) = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathrm{Cov}(e) = \sigma^2 I_n .$$ Equivalently \(\mathrm{E}(Y) = X\beta\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(Y) = \sigma^2 I\). Unpacked, the second condition bundles two separate claims: \(\mathrm{Var}(e_i) = \sigma^2\) for all \(i\) (homoscedasticity) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(e_i,e_j)=0\) for \(i\ne j\) (uncorrelatedness).
What is not assumed. No normality, no independence (only zero correlation), no distributional form whatsoever. Everything in §§5–8 holds at this level of generality. Normality enters the course later, and buys different things: exact \(t\) and \(F\) distributions, and the stronger claim that least squares is best among all unbiased estimators rather than merely the linear ones.

5.1 Moments of the least squares quantities

Proposition 5.1 Under the GM assumptions: $$\mathrm{E}(\hat Y) = X\beta,\qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\hat Y) = \sigma^2 M,$$ $$\mathrm{E}(\hat e) = 0,\qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\hat e) = \sigma^2 (I-M),\qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\hat Y, \hat e) = 0 .$$
Proof \(\mathrm{E}(MY) = M X\beta = X\beta\) using \(MX = X\), and \(\mathrm{Cov}(MY) = M(\sigma^2 I)M' = \sigma^2 M^2 = \sigma^2 M\). Similarly \(\mathrm{E}[(I-M)Y] = (I-M)X\beta = 0\) since \((I-M)X = 0\), and \(\mathrm{Cov}[(I-M)Y] = \sigma^2 (I-M)\). The cross-covariance is \(\mathrm{Cov}(MY,(I-M)Y) = \sigma^2 M(I-M)' = \sigma^2 M(I-M) = 0\).
Note the asymmetry. \(\mathrm{E}(\hat Y) = X\beta\) always. But \(\mathrm{E}(\hat\beta) = (X'X)^-X'X\beta\), which equals \(\beta\) only when \(r(X)=p\). In the rank-deficient case \(\hat\beta\) is a biased estimator of \(\beta\) — indeed \(\beta\) is not even well defined. Again: work with \(\hat Y\), or with estimable functions.

6. Estimability and identifiability

Definition Estimable function A linear parametric function \(\lambda'\beta\) is estimable if there exists a vector \(\rho\) such that \(\mathrm{E}(\rho'Y) = \lambda'\beta\) for every \(\beta\) — that is, if it admits a linear unbiased estimator.
Theorem 6.1 Characterisation of estimability \(\lambda'\beta\) is estimable \(\iff\) \(\lambda' = \rho'X\) for some \(\rho\) \(\iff\) \(\lambda \in C(X')\).
Proof \(\mathrm{E}(\rho'Y) = \rho'X\beta\). Requiring \(\rho'X\beta = \lambda'\beta\) for all \(\beta\) forces \(\rho'X = \lambda'\), i.e. \(\lambda = X'\rho \in C(X')\). Conversely any \(\lambda = X'\rho\) gives the unbiased estimator \(\rho'Y\).
Corollary 6.2 If \(r(X) = p\), every \(\lambda'\beta\) is estimable, since then \(C(X') = \mathbb{R}^p\).

Identifiability is the companion notion: \(\beta\) is identifiable if \(X\beta_1 = X\beta_2 \Rightarrow \beta_1 = \beta_2\), i.e. if \(N(X) = \{0\}\), i.e. if \(r(X)=p\). When \(\beta\) is not identifiable, distinct parameter vectors give identical distributions for \(Y\), so no amount of data can separate them. Estimability is precisely the condition that the particular combination \(\lambda'\beta\) is constant across all \(\beta\) yielding the same \(X\beta\):

Proposition 6.3 \(\lambda'\beta\) is estimable iff \(\lambda'\beta_1 = \lambda'\beta_2\) whenever \(X\beta_1 = X\beta_2\).
Proof (\(\Rightarrow\)) If \(\lambda' = \rho'X\) and \(X\beta_1 = X\beta_2\), then \(\lambda'\beta_1 = \rho'X\beta_1 = \rho'X\beta_2 = \lambda'\beta_2\). (\(\Leftarrow\)) Suppose \(\lambda'\) is constant on the cosets. \(X\beta_1 = X\beta_2\) iff \(\beta_1 - \beta_2 \in N(X)\), so the hypothesis says \(\lambda' d = 0\) for all \(d \in N(X)\), i.e. \(\lambda \in N(X)^\perp = C(X')\), which is estimability by Theorem 6.1.

7. The Gauss–Markov theorem

This is the central result of the week, and the justification for using least squares at all.

Theorem 7.1 Gauss–Markov Assume the GM conditions and let \(\lambda'\beta\) be estimable. Let \(\hat\beta\) be any solution of the normal equations. Then \(\lambda'\hat\beta\) is the unique best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) of \(\lambda'\beta\): among all estimators \(a'Y\) with \(\mathrm{E}(a'Y) = \lambda'\beta\) for all \(\beta\), it has strictly smallest variance.
Proof

Since \(\lambda'\beta\) is estimable, write \(\lambda' = \rho'X\). Then for any solution \(\hat\beta\) of the normal equations, \(X\hat\beta = MY\), so

$$\lambda'\hat\beta = \rho'X\hat\beta = \rho'MY = u'Y, \qquad u := M\rho .$$

Two things follow immediately. First, \(\lambda'\hat\beta\) is the same number for every solution \(\hat\beta\) — the non-uniqueness of \(\hat\beta\) is invisible in estimable functions. Second, it is a linear estimator with coefficient vector \(u = M\rho \in C(X)\).

Unbiasedness. \(\mathrm{E}(u'Y) = \rho'MX\beta = \rho'X\beta = \lambda'\beta\), using \(MX=X\).

Optimality. Let \(a'Y\) be any linear unbiased estimator, so \(a'X\beta = \lambda'\beta\) for all \(\beta\), i.e. \(a'X = \lambda' = \rho'X\). Also \(u'X = \rho'MX = \rho'X\). Subtracting,

$$(a-u)'X = 0, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad a - u \in C(X)^\perp .$$

Since \(u \in C(X)\), we get \(u'(a-u) = 0\). Now decompose \(a'Y = u'Y + (a-u)'Y\). The two pieces are uncorrelated:

$$\mathrm{Cov}\big(u'Y, (a-u)'Y\big) = \sigma^2 u'(a-u) = 0 ,$$

so variances add:

$$\mathrm{Var}(a'Y) = \mathrm{Var}(u'Y) + \mathrm{Var}\big((a-u)'Y\big) = \mathrm{Var}(u'Y) + \sigma^2\|a-u\|^2 \;\ge\; \mathrm{Var}(u'Y).$$

Uniqueness. Equality holds iff \(\sigma^2\|a-u\|^2 = 0\) iff \(a = u\) (assuming \(\sigma^2>0\)). So the BLUE is unique as an estimator.

Corollary 7.2 Variance of the BLUE With \(\lambda' = \rho'X\), \(\;\mathrm{Var}(\lambda'\hat\beta) = \sigma^2\rho'M\rho\). If \(r(X)=p\), this is \(\sigma^2 \lambda'(X'X)^{-1}\lambda\), and \(\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\beta) = \sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}\).
What the theorem does and does not say. It is a statement about linear estimators only, and it needs only two moments. Drop homoscedasticity or uncorrelatedness and least squares stays unbiased but stops being best — that is the motivation for generalized least squares later in the course. Add normality and the conclusion strengthens: \(\lambda'\hat\beta\) becomes the minimum variance estimator among all unbiased estimators, linear or not.

8. Estimating \(\sigma^2\) and degrees of freedom

Define the error (residual) sum of squares and the mean squared error:

$$\mathrm{SSE} = \|\hat e\|^2 = Y'(I-M)Y, \qquad \mathrm{MSE} = \frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n-r}, \qquad r = r(X).$$
Theorem 8.1 Under the GM assumptions, \(\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{SSE}) = \sigma^2 (n-r)\), so \(\mathrm{MSE}\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).
Proof Apply Lemma 3.1 with \(A = I-M\), \(\mu = X\beta\), \(V = \sigma^2 I\): $$\mathrm{E}\big(Y'(I-M)Y\big) = \mathrm{tr}\big((I-M)\sigma^2 I\big) + \beta'X'(I-M)X\beta .$$ The second term vanishes because \((I-M)X = 0\). The first is \(\sigma^2\,\mathrm{tr}(I-M) = \sigma^2 (n-r)\) by Corollary 2.3.

So the divisor \(n-r\) is not a convention — it is exactly what unbiasedness requires, and it equals \(\dim C(X)^\perp\), the dimension of the space the residual vector is free to live in. In simple linear regression \(r=2\) and we recover the familiar \(n-2\).

9. Worked example: the one-way classification

Example 9.1 Where estimability bites

Consider \(t\) treatments with \(n_i\) observations each, \(N = \sum_i n_i\):

$$y_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_i + e_{ij}, \qquad i=1,\dots,t,\quad j = 1,\dots,n_i .$$

Here \(\beta = (\mu, \alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_t)'\) has \(p = t+1\) entries, and each row of \(X\) has the form \((1, 0,\dots,1,\dots,0)\) with the \(1\) in position \(i+1\). The first column of \(X\) is the sum of the remaining \(t\) columns, so

$$r(X) = t < t+1 = p .$$

The model is rank deficient: \(X'X\) is singular and \(\hat\beta\) is not unique. Which functions are estimable? By Theorem 6.1 we need \(\lambda \in C(X')\), and \(C(X')\) is spanned by the distinct rows, i.e. by the vectors \((1, e_i')\). A general element is

$$\sum_i c_i (1, e_i') = \Big(\textstyle\sum_i c_i,\; c_1,\dots,c_t\Big).$$

So, writing \(\lambda = (\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_t)'\):

$$\boxed{\;\lambda'\beta \text{ is estimable} \iff \lambda_0 = \sum_{i=1}^t \lambda_i .\;}$$

Applying the criterion:

Function\(\lambda_0\) vs \(\sum\lambda_i\)Estimable?
\(\mu + \alpha_1\)\(1 = 1\)Yes — the treatment-1 cell mean
\(\alpha_1 - \alpha_2\)\(0 = 1-1\)Yes — a contrast
\(\alpha_1\)\(0 \ne 1\)No
\(\mu\)\(1 \ne 0\)No

The pattern is the general one: individual treatment effects are meaningless without a side condition, but contrasts \(\sum c_i\alpha_i\) with \(\sum c_i = 0\), and cell means \(\mu+\alpha_i\), are always estimable. Their BLUEs are the obvious things — \(\widehat{\mu+\alpha_i} = \bar y_{i\cdot}\) and \(\widehat{\alpha_1-\alpha_2} = \bar y_{1\cdot} - \bar y_{2\cdot}\) — and Theorem 7.1 certifies that nothing linear and unbiased does better.

Constraints such as \(\sum_i n_i\alpha_i = 0\) or \(\alpha_t=0\) (R's default) make \(\hat\beta\) unique, but they are bookkeeping choices, not information. They change what the individual \(\hat\alpha_i\) print as; they cannot change \(\hat Y = MY\), nor the estimate of any estimable function.

10.1 Projection is what lm computes

set.seed(1)
n <- 20
x <- runif(n, 0, 10)
y <- 3 + 1.5 * x + rnorm(n)

fit <- lm(y ~ x)
X   <- model.matrix(fit)              # n x 2, full rank
M   <- X %*% solve(t(X) %*% X) %*% t(X)   # the ppo onto C(X)

# Theorem 4.1: fitted values are the projection MY
all.equal(as.vector(M %*% y), unname(fitted(fit)))   # TRUE

# Theorem 2.1: M is symmetric and idempotent
all.equal(M, t(M)); all.equal(M %*% M, M)            # TRUE, TRUE

# Corollary 2.3 / "rank = trace": tr(M) = r(X) = 2
sum(diag(M)); qr(X)$rank                             # 2, 2
n - sum(diag(M)); df.residual(fit)                   # 18, 18

10.2 Algebraic properties of residuals (Proposition 4.3)

e <- resid(fit)
sum(e)                      # ~0  : intercept present, property (2)
crossprod(X, e)             # ~0  : property (1), X'e = 0
crossprod(fitted(fit), e)   # ~0  : property (3)

# Pythagoras, property (4)
all.equal(sum(y^2), sum(fitted(fit)^2) + sum(e^2))   # TRUE

# ...but drop the intercept and (2) fails
sum(resid(lm(y ~ x - 1)))   # NOT zero

10.3 Rank deficiency and estimability (Example 9.1)

grp <- factor(rep(1:3, each = 5))
z   <- rnorm(15, mean = rep(c(2, 5, 9), each = 5))

# Overparametrised design: mu, alpha_1, alpha_2, alpha_3
Xo <- cbind(1, model.matrix(~ grp - 1))
qr(Xo)$rank          # 3, not 4 -- rank deficient

library(MASS)
b1 <- as.vector(ginv(t(Xo) %*% Xo) %*% t(Xo) %*% z)   # one solution
b2 <- unname(c(0, tapply(z, grp, mean)))              # another, with mu = 0

round(cbind(b1, b2), 4)
#          b1     b2          the two solutions disagree completely
#   [1,] 4.0677 0.0000
#   [2,] -2.0252 2.0425
#   [3,] 1.1312 5.1989
#   [4,] 4.9617 9.0294

all.equal(as.vector(Xo %*% b1), as.vector(Xo %*% b2))   # TRUE -- identical fit

# Non-estimable alpha_1 differs between solutions...
b1[2]; b2[2]                                  # -2.0252  vs  2.0425

# ...but the estimable contrast alpha_1 - alpha_2 agrees exactly:
all.equal(b1[2] - b1[3], b2[2] - b2[3])       # TRUE  (both -3.156381)

(Use unname() as above: tapply returns a named vector, and all.equal reports a mismatch on the names attribute even when the numbers agree to machine precision.)

That last block is the whole lesson of §6 in six lines: two different \(\hat\beta\), the same \(\hat Y\), and agreement exactly on the estimable functions.

11. Self-check exercises

Exercises
  1. Show that if \(M\) is the ppo onto \(C(X)\) and \(C(X_0)\subseteq C(X)\) with ppo \(M_0\), then \(MM_0 = M_0M = M_0\). (This is the engine behind the \(F\)-test for nested models.)
  2. Prove that \(M\) is the ppo onto \(C(X)\) iff \(M\) is idempotent, symmetric, \(MX = X\) and \(C(M)\subseteq C(X)\).
  3. Verify directly that \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \sigma^2/S_{xx}\) in Example 4.4, both from \(\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}\) and from writing \(\hat\beta_1 = \sum c_i y_i\) explicitly.
  4. In Example 9.1, show \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar y_{1\cdot} - \bar y_{2\cdot}) = \sigma^2(1/n_1 + 1/n_2)\) and confirm this matches Corollary 7.2.
  5. Suppose \(\mathrm{Cov}(Y) = \sigma^2 V\) with \(V\) known and positive definite, not \(\sigma^2 I\). Show \(\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}}\) is still unbiased for estimable functions but no longer BLUE, by exhibiting the variance of a competing linear unbiased estimator. (This motivates §2.7 of Christensen.)
  6. Show that \(\mathrm{SSE}\) can be written \(Y'Y - \hat\beta'X'Y\) for any solution \(\hat\beta\) of the normal equations, and explain why the expression does not depend on which solution is used.
